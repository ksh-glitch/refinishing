The Workshop Journal · Polish & silicone

Put down the Pledge.

Nearly every aerosol furniture polish works the same way: it leaves a film of silicone oil on the surface. The shine is instant, which is the entire appeal. What the can does not mention is that silicone never dries, never stops moving, and never entirely comes off — and that the people who pay for it are the ones who refinish the piece thirty years later. Usually that is us.

01 — What the Can Is SellingShine ≠ finish

The first misunderstanding

The shine is not the finish.

When a polished tabletop gleams, most people assume they are looking at the wood. They are looking at whatever was sprayed on it that morning.

A finished piece already has its surface — shellac, lacquer, varnish, oil, or a French polish built up by hand over days. That film is the finish. It is what protects the wood, and it is what gives a good piece its depth: light travels down into it and comes back up through the grain. A silicone spray sits on top of that film and contributes nothing to it. What it returns is a flat, glassy reflection that stops at the surface. Set a sprayed panel beside a waxed one and the difference is not subtle.

The other claim on the can is that the product feeds, nourishes or moisturizes the wood. It does not, and it cannot. The wood is sealed under the finish and has been since the day the piece was made; nothing wiped on the outside reaches it. Old wood is kept healthy by the air around it — steady humidity, no radiator, no afternoon sun — never by anything sold in an aerosol.

02 — Where It GoesIt never dries

Migration and buildup

Silicone doesn’t dry. It travels.

A finish cures. Wax hardens. Silicone oil does neither — it stays liquid for the life of the piece, and liquids go where they are allowed.

What they are allowed is every opening the surface offers: the open pores of oak and mahogany, the fine crazing in an old shellac finish, the hairline gaps around inlay and banding, the seam where a top meets its edge. Once it is down in there, no amount of wiping brings it back. Each application adds to what is already below.

Above the surface the effect is more familiar. The film is oily, so it collects dust instead of shedding it, which is much of why a heavily polished piece seems to need polishing again so soon — the shine is being restored to the polish, not to the furniture. Over the years the layers go cloudy and streaked, worst where hands land: the front edge of a table, the arms of a chair, the top of a chest. Owners tend to blame the piece. It is the polish.

03 — FisheyeThe bench evidence

What a restorer sees

The craters have a name.

Strip a silicone-soaked piece to bare wood, lay on the first coat of new finish, and watch it retreat into small round craters, each with a dot at its center. The trade calls this fisheye. It is the reason finishers dislike silicone the way they dislike nothing else.

The mechanism is plain physics. A finish wets a surface evenly only where the surface tension is even. Silicone lowers it sharply wherever it sits, so the wet finish flows away from those spots the way water beads off a waxed car. The result is not a blemish to be buffed out afterwards. It is a coat that has refused to lie down.

After that it becomes a fight. The wood is washed and degreased. A barrier coat of dewaxed shellac goes down to seal in whatever remains. Sometimes an additive goes into the finish itself — which is, with some irony, also a silicone, working on the principle that if the surface tension cannot be raised everywhere it can at least be lowered everywhere. Any of it can work. None of it is guaranteed on wood that has been sprayed weekly for thirty years, and all of it is time spent on a job that never needed to take that time.

Which is the honest reason for this note. Silicone will not visibly damage your furniture this year, or next. It makes the eventual restoration slower, dearer and less certain — and that bill is paid by whoever inherits the piece.

04 — Reading the LabelWhat to look for

Polish is not the enemy

The word to look for is silicone.

A good cream polish is a genuinely useful thing to own. The trouble is a single ingredient, and it is worth learning to spot — formulations differ between products and change over time, so the label is a better guide than the brand.

Furniture polishes are not required to list everything in them, which makes the shortcut useful: if a can promises a lasting shine from a two-second spray, something is being left behind to produce it.

  • Silicone, by any name
    Look for silicone itself, and for the -siloxane and -methicone endings — dimethicone and polydimethylsiloxane are the same material wearing a longer word.
  • “Silicone-free” aerosols
    Better, not good. Most are largely solvent and mineral oil. They still build up, still hold dust, and still have to be cleaned off before finish work begins.
  • “Lemon oil”
    In almost every case, mineral oil with a scent. It will not ruin anything. It also does very little — it leaves a surface looking wet rather than finished, and then gathers dust.
  • Kitchen oils
    Never olive or vegetable oil on furniture. They do not dry, they go rancid, and the smell comes back out of the grain for years.
05 — What to Use InsteadA cloth, mostly

The whole routine

It is mostly a cloth.

  1. Dust with cotton, and nothing on it

    A soft cotton cloth, dry, is the entire weekly programme. Where there is a film that dry cotton will not lift, dampen the cloth with water, wring it until it is barely cool to the touch, wipe a small area and follow immediately with a dry cloth. Water is not the enemy of a finish; standing water is.

  2. Answer spills at once

    Blot, do not wipe — wiping spreads a spill into an area instead of lifting it. Almost every white ring we are asked about began as a glass that sat for an hour on an unprotected top.

  3. Cream polish twice a year, not twice a week

    A quality silicone-free cream polish carries a mild cleaner and a little wax in emulsion: it lifts the grime of ordinary use and leaves a thin protective film with some depth to it. Twice a year is plenty. More often and you are back to building something up. The one we keep on the bench is on our care kit page.

  4. Paste wax, if the finish suits it

    On shellac, lacquer and varnish, a hard paste wax is the traditional protection and it is excellent — a thin coat, left to haze, buffed off. Two cautions. It is a commitment, since wax has to come off again before some finish work. And it does not belong on an oiled finish. If you are not sure which you have, ask before you start.

  5. Mind the room, not the surface

    Far more furniture is hurt by dry winter air, a nearby vent and afternoon sun than by anything in a bottle. Keeping a piece off an exterior wall and out of the light does more for it than a decade of polishing. Our care guide covers the habits in full.

06 — If You’ve Been Using ItNo harm done yet

What to do now

Stop, clean, and tell us.

Nobody needs to feel caught out. The stuff has been advertised for seventy years and sold beside the paper towels, and very nearly every piece that arrives at this shop has had some.

The buildup itself usually comes off with a cotton cloth dampened in warm water and a drop of dish soap, wrung out until it is barely damp, worked in small sections and dried straight away. Repeat rather than scrub. Where it is stubborn, a cloth barely damp with mineral spirits will do it — but test somewhere hidden first, and keep it away from shellac, and from anything painted or gilded.

Then say something. This is the part that matters more than the cleaning. If a piece is going to a restorer, tell them what has been used on it and for how long. It changes how the work is prepared: a shop that knows seals for it from the start. A shop that does not know finds out when the first coat craters, and by then a day has been lost by everyone.

And have the conversation with whoever cleans the house. Cleaners reach for the aerosol because it is quick, because it makes a room read as cared for, and because nobody has ever told them otherwise. It is not a difficult conversation — these pieces get dusted with a dry cotton cloth, nothing is sprayed on them, and here is the polish for twice a year. Most people are glad of a clear rule.

07 — QuestionsAsked at the bench

Straight answers

What clients ask.

Is Pledge bad for wood furniture?

The concern is silicone, which is used in many aerosol furniture polishes to produce an instant shine. Formulations differ between products and change over time, so read the label rather than trusting a brand name. Silicone will not visibly harm a finish this year: the problem is that it never dries, so it holds dust and builds into a smeary film, and it soaks into pores, crazing and seams where wiping cannot reach it. The bill arrives years later. When the piece is refinished, silicone makes the new finish pull away in small craters — fisheye — and the wood has to be washed, sealed and sometimes fought before a coat will lie down. For furniture you intend to keep, dust with a dry cotton cloth and use a silicone-free cream polish once or twice a year instead.

What furniture polish do professional restorers use?

Far less than people expect. Day to day the answer is a soft cotton cloth, dry or barely damp and followed immediately by a dry one. Once or twice a year we use a quality silicone-free cream polish to lift the grime of ordinary use, or a hard paste wax where the finish suits it — thin coat, let it haze, buff it off. What we do not use on anything is an aerosol. The products we actually keep on the bench are listed on our care kit page.

I’ve used spray polish for years. Have I ruined my furniture?

Almost certainly not. Silicone is a refinishing complication rather than a destructive one, and the finish and wood underneath are usually sound. Take the buildup off with a cotton cloth dampened in warm water with a drop of dish soap, wrung out until it is barely damp, worked in small sections and dried straight away — repeat rather than scrub. Then switch to dusting and a twice-yearly cream polish. The one thing that genuinely matters: when the piece eventually goes to a restorer, say what has been used on it and for how long. A shop that knows will seal for it from the start.

Does furniture polish feed or nourish wood?

No. The wood in a finished piece is sealed beneath shellac, lacquer, varnish or oil and has been since the day it was made, so nothing wiped on the outside reaches it. What keeps old wood healthy is the air around it — steady humidity, away from radiators, vents and direct sun. A good polish cleans the finish and leaves a thin protective film, which is a real and useful job. It is not feeding.
