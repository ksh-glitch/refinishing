Furniture care · The short list

The kit we keep at the bench.

Most of the damage that reaches this workshop was done slowly, at home, by the wrong product used with the best intentions. Four things cover nearly everything a good piece will ask of you between professional visits. This is the list we give clients when they collect a restored piece.

01 — The KitFour things, and that is most of it

What we actually use

Four items, one drawer.

Not a shopping list — a working kit. Between them these handle routine care, the scratch that appears overnight, the small structural repair, and a finish that has gone thirsty.

  1. A cream polish, used sparingly

    Once or twice a year — not weekly. A quality cream polish lifts the buildup that regular use leaves behind and brings back lustre on any fine finish, kitchen cabinets included. Between polishings, a barely damp cotton cloth is the entire routine. More polish is not better care; it is the most common mistake we see.

    Find it on Amazon ↗

  2. Touch-up markers

    Scratches happen and corners get bumped. A marker close to your piece’s colour makes a shallow scratch disappear from normal viewing distance in about a minute. Choose a shade slightly lighter than you think you need — you can always go darker, and going lighter means calling us.

    Find them on Amazon ↗

  3. An epoxy putty stick

    For a stripped screw hole, a small crack, or a missing corner on a piece that does not warrant a trip to the bench. It is a sound, honest repair for furniture in daily service. It is not for anything with veneer, inlay or real value — those we would rather see first.

    Find it on Amazon ↗

  4. Boiled linseed oil, for thirsty finishes

    An oil-finished piece that has gone dry and chalky often needs feeding rather than refinishing. Test an inconspicuous spot first, apply thinly, and wipe off everything that does not absorb. Note that oily rags can spontaneously combust — spread them flat outdoors to dry before disposal.

02 — The Other ListWhat we undo, week after week

Keep these away from a fine finish

The products that pay our mortgage.

We have no affiliate arrangement on anything in this section, for the obvious reason. This is simply what we spend our weeks undoing.

  • Silicone spray polishes. The supermarket aerosols. They shine beautifully and leave a residue that migrates into the wood; when the piece eventually needs refinishing, that silicone causes fisheye — craters in the new finish — and the shop has to fight it. House cleaners love the stuff. Have a conversation with yours about what goes on your good pieces — and if you want the whole account, we wrote it out in Put down the Pledge.
  • Anything labelled “restorer” or “scratch remover” that promises miracles. Most are dye plus oil. They darken and blotch, and they complicate honest repair later.
  • Sandpaper, on a white ring. A white mark is usually moisture trapped in the finish, not damage to the wood. Sand it and you turn a reversible problem into a refinish.
  • Wet cloths and household sprays. Water sitting in a joint or seam does slow, structural damage. Damp, never wet.
  • Dipping and stripping services for anything you care about. Tanks blur carving, lift veneer and bleach old wood. It is the reason we strip by hand and never in a tank.
03 — When to StopThe line between care and repair

Put the kit down

Four times to call instead of trying.

  1. Veneer, inlay or marquetry is involved

    Anything laid over a substrate lifts, and lifting veneer gets worse with heat, moisture and pressure. Home remedies usually enlarge the repair.

  2. The mark is dark, not white

    White generally lives in the finish and is often reversible. Dark means the moisture reached the wood, and that is bench work.

  3. The piece wobbles

    A loose joint is tired glue, not a failure — but gluing over old glue is glue on glue, not glue on wood. It has to come apart first to come back properly.

  4. The piece may be valuable

    If it might be genuinely old or rare, do nothing and send photographs. On the right piece the original surface is worth more than any improvement, and we will tell you when that is the case.
