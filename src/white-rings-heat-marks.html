The Workshop Journal · White rings & heat marks

The ring is not in the wood.

A glass sat somewhere for an hour, or a dish came out of the oven and went straight onto the table, and now there is a pale cloud where neither should be. Almost every one of those marks is moisture caught inside the finish, and the wood underneath has not been touched. That is the whole reason so many of them come out. Here is what to try, in order of risk — and, more usefully, how to know when to leave it alone.

01 — What It Actually IsMoisture, caught

The good news first

A mark in the film, not the timber.

The finish on a table is a thin transparent film lying over the wood. When moisture gets into that film and cannot get back out, it stops being clear: light scatters off the trapped water instead of passing through, and what you see is white.

That is the entire mechanism, and it is why a white ring is better news than it looks. The mark sits in a layer that can be persuaded to let go of what is in it. The wood below — the grain, the colour, the value — is exactly as it was the day before.

A heat mark is the same event with the heat supplied by a dish rather than the room. Finishers call the clouding blush, and it turns up wherever warm damp air meets a cool film: under a glass, under a vase, along the edge of a place setting, in a stripe where a pizza box sat.

Which finish you have decides how easily it reverses. Shellac and lacquer soften and re-flow, so they respond well. Modern polyurethane and catalysed finishes do not re-flow at all, which makes them harder to mark in the first place and more stubborn when they do.

02 — White or DarkThe one test that matters

Before you try anything

Look at the colour.

White, cloudy or hazy means the moisture stopped at the finish. Dark, grey or black means it did not.

A dark ring is water that has gone through the film and into the wood, where it has either reacted with the tannins in the timber or arrived with mildew. Neither comes out with heat, and neither comes out with polishing — you would be working on a layer the problem is no longer in. Getting it out means taking the finish off and treating the stain underneath, and that is bench work.

The chest in the plate is what standing water does when it is given long enough: the staining had gone well past the finish, and it came out only with a full strip and refinish. Nothing that could be done in a living room would have touched it.

Two other things say stop before you start. If the finish is lifting, flaking or tacky, the film has failed rather than blushed, and heat will make a bigger mess of it. And if you can feel the mark with a fingertip as well as see it, the finish itself has moved — softened and imprinted — which is a repair rather than a rescue.

None of that means the piece is in trouble. It means the fix belongs to somebody with the solvents to do it. If you are weighing whether a mark is worth a claim rather than a cloth, our damage and insurance page covers how that works.

03 — What to TryIn order of risk

On an ordinary piece, in this order

Least dangerous first.

  1. Do nothing for a fortnight

    The most underused remedy there is. A great many fresh rings simply leave on their own as the moisture works its way back out of the film. Move the piece somewhere dry and reasonably warm, keep it out of direct sun, and look again in two weeks. It costs nothing and it cannot go wrong, which is more than can be said for anything below it on this list.

  2. Gentle, dry heat

    A dry iron on its lowest setting — never steam, which adds the exact thing you are trying to remove — over a clean, dry, folded cotton cloth. Two or three seconds, lift, look. Two or three more, lift, look. Never let it stand. A hairdryer on low, held a few inches off, is slower and considerably safer, and often does the same job. Stop the moment the mark begins to change: you can always do more, and you cannot undo. Too hot or too long turns something reversible into something permanent.

  3. A very fine abrasive, with oil

    If heat has taken the ring down and a ghost of it remains, a little mineral oil on a soft cloth with an extremely fine abrasive will polish the top few microns of the finish away. The traditional shop version is oil and pumice. Understand what it is: you are removing finish, deliberately, and you will dull the sheen in that patch, so the work has to be blended outward into the surrounding surface. This is the last step you should take at home and the first one that goes wrong quietly.

† The kitchen remedies — toothpaste, baking soda, cigarette ash — are all step three with uncontrolled grit and no way to know how coarse it is. On a finish you care about, that is not a saving.

04 — What Not to DoUndone weekly, at this bench

The five that reach us

Each of these makes it worse.

A white ring is a small job. Most of the large jobs that start as white rings were made large in the hour after somebody looked it up.

  • Sandpaper
    Even a fine grit goes through a finish far faster than anyone expects, and on veneer it goes through the veneer. Modern veneer can be under a millimetre; the hand-cut veneer on period furniture is thicker but still gives you very little to lose. This is how a reversible mark becomes a refinish.
  • Steam, or a steam iron
    The mark is trapped moisture. Adding moisture is the one intervention guaranteed to work against you.
  • A heat gun
    Built to strip finishes, and very good at it. There is no low setting that makes this a repair tool.
  • Mayonnaise or petroleum jelly, overnight
    Largely harmless, and largely useless. The oil seeps in and darkens the surface so the ring reads as less visible; as it migrates, the ring comes back. What you have added is something the next person has to take off.
  • Alcohol, on shellac
    Alcohol dissolves shellac. Re-flowing a shellac finish with it is a real technique and a good one — it is also a technique with about a two-second margin, and it is how a ring becomes a bare patch.
05 — When Not to Touch ItThe judgment

Five pieces to leave alone

Some tables are not the place to learn.

Everything above assumes a piece where the worst case is an afternoon lost. Put the iron down if any of the following is true.

It is veneered, inlaid or has marquetry. The film is thin and what lies under it is thinner. It is shellac or French polish. Heat softens it and alcohol dissolves it, and on an antique that surface is a great deal of what the piece is worth. The surface is original and undisturbed. Patina is not a look; it is value, and it does not grow back. You do not know which finish you have. That is not a small gap — the finish decides the method, and identifying it is the first thing done at a bench, with solvents, in a hidden place. The piece matters to you more than the afternoon does. That one is not technical, and it is the one that decides most cases correctly.

What happens here instead is rarely dramatic. The finish is identified, the blush is drawn out or the film re-flowed with the right solvent, or fresh finish is padded in over that area alone, and the sheen is blended so the repair does not read as a patch. It is a spot repair, not a refinish, and it costs a fraction of one — which is exactly why it is worth asking before reaching for the sandpaper rather than after.

* Prevention is duller and cheaper: coasters, felt under anything that lands on a good top, a trivet for hot dishes, and blotting spills rather than wiping them. The rest of the habits are in our furniture care guide.

06 — QuestionsAsked at the bench

Straight answers

What clients ask.

How do you get a white ring out of a wood table?

A white ring is moisture trapped inside the finish rather than damage to the wood, so the aim is to let that moisture out. Try three things in order of risk. First, wait: many fresh rings fade on their own over a week or two in a dry, warm room. Second, gentle dry heat — a dry iron on its lowest setting, never steam, over a folded dry cotton cloth, in two-second passes with a look between each. Third, if a ghost remains, a little mineral oil on a soft cloth with a very fine abrasive, which polishes the top of the finish and will also dull the sheen in that patch. Stop at the first sign of change; you can always do more. On veneer, marquetry, shellac or French polish, or any piece whose original surface is part of its value, do none of this — a spot repair at a bench costs a fraction of the refinish a home attempt can cause.

What is the difference between a white ring and a black ring?

It is the difference between a mark in the finish and a mark in the wood. White or cloudy means moisture is caught in the finish film, scattering light — the wood beneath is untouched, and the mark can often be reversed without refinishing. Dark, grey or black means water got through the finish and into the wood, where it has either reacted with the tannins or brought mildew with it. That does not come out with heat or polishing. It needs the finish removed and the stain treated, which is bench work. A dark ring is a reason to stop and send photographs rather than to experiment.

Does the iron trick actually work?

Often, on the right finish, done carefully. Heat lets the finish relax so the trapped moisture can leave, which works best on shellac and lacquer because those films soften and re-flow. Modern polyurethane and catalysed finishes do not re-flow, so results there are far less reliable. The rules that matter: a dry iron only, never steam, on the lowest setting, over a clean dry cotton cloth, two or three seconds at a time with a look in between, and never left standing. Too hot or too long turns a reversible mark into a permanent one. And do not try it at all on veneer, marquetry, shellac or French polish.

Can a heat mark from a hot dish be removed?

Usually, because a heat mark is the same thing as a water ring: moisture driven into the finish, clouding it. The same order applies — wait, then gentle dry heat, then a very fine abrasive with oil if a ghost remains. Where a heat mark differs is that severe ones also soften the film, so the surface may be slightly deformed or imprinted as well as clouded. If you can feel the mark with a fingertip rather than only see it, the finish itself has moved and it is bench work. Send photographs and we will tell you which one you have.
